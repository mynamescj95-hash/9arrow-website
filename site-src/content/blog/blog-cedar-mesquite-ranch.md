Clearing cedar and mesquite is how a lot of Texas ranches get their grass back. Both species creep across pasture, crowd out forage and make the land harder to work. They respond to clearing in very different ways, though. Ashe juniper dies when it is cut low. Mesquite fights back from the roots. A good plan treats the two differently.

This guide covers how we approach ranch land that has gone to cedar and mesquite, and how to keep it open once it is cleared.

## Why clearing cedar and mesquite takes two different plans

Ashe juniper, the Hill Country cedar, is a non-sprouting species. Mulch it below the lowest green branch and the tree is gone. What comes back afterward is new seedlings.

Mesquite is built to survive damage. Young mesquite forms an underground bud zone near the base of the stem, and when the top is cut, burned or shredded, new shoots come up from that zone. Texas A&M research notes that decades of top-kill treatments have turned many stands of few-stemmed mesquite into multi-stemmed resprout thickets.

Mulching cedar and mulching mesquite therefore give two different results. Cedar stays gone. Mesquite comes back unless the bud zone is removed or the regrowth is managed.

## How cedar and mesquite take over ranch pasture

Brush encroachment rarely happens overnight. Texas A&M research points to overgrazing and the long absence of natural fire as major reasons juniper spreads. Birds carry cedar berries into open pasture. Cattle and wildlife, including deer, coyotes and feral hogs, eat mesquite beans and spread the seed in their droppings.

As brush thickens, grass loses light and water. Texas A&M research found that dense Ashe juniper canopy and leaf litter catch much of the rain before it reaches the soil, and cedar breaks shade out forage. Pastures that once carried cattle turn into thickets you cannot walk or see through. For more on the cedar side, read [how to stop cedar regrowth](blog-clear-cedar-without-regrowth) after clearing.

## Clearing cedar on ranch land

Forestry mulching fits cedar on ranch ground. The mulcher grinds each tree down at the base, takes dense stands in one operation, and leaves the root balls in place to hold the soil. The mulch covers the ground, and there are no burn piles to manage during a burn ban.

Many ranches also carry old bulldozer piles from earlier clearing. We mulch those piles too, even piles as large as a house, and turn them back into usable ground.

## Clearing mesquite for lasting results

Mesquite needs a decision up front. Two paths work, and many ranches use both.

### Grub the mesquite root crown

Grubbing pulls the tree and its root crown out below the bud zone. Texas A&M AgriLife Extension calls grubbing a long-term control option and warns that leaving any part of the bud zone can let the tree resprout. We grub with bulldozers and excavators, and we prefer to mulch those root balls on site.

### Mulch mesquite and manage the regrowth

Mulching mesquite at ground level clears the pasture and keeps the soil intact, but expect resprouts. Mulching works when you plan follow-up from the start, such as periodic shredding to keep sprouts short. Many landowners also talk with Texas A&M AgriLife Extension about treatment options that fit their goals.

A common mix is to grub mesquite in the pastures that matter most and to mulch and manage the rest.

## Selective clearing keeps the trees that matter

A cleared pasture does not have to be a bare one. Live oaks and other shade trees give cattle relief in a Texas summer, hold soil along draws and add character to the land. Before work starts, we walk the ranch with you and flag the trees that stay.

Oak wilt is common in Central Texas. Oak wilt spreads in defined paths, and infected trees cannot be restored. Selective clearing is often the best option for protecting the healthy oaks around a problem area, and Texas A&M Forest Service advises avoiding pruning or wounding oaks from February through June.

## Timing ranch clearing around grass and oaks

Clearing pasture ahead of spring green-up lets grass take advantage of the rain. Under a light layer of mulch, grass can green up within days after a good rain. Under a heavy layer, up to 12 inches deep, grass can take two to three years to come through, so mulch depth is worth discussing before the work starts.

Firm, dry ground keeps heavy machines from rutting pasture soil, so weather shapes the schedule on big jobs. Work near keeper oaks fits best from late fall through January, outside the February through June oak wilt window.

## Keeping ranch pasture open after clearing

Clearing is the big step. Keeping the land open is the long one.

Periodic shredding with a tractor and batwing mower knocks back mesquite sprouts and young cedar before they get established. Ranch and rural properties are scheduled by season and conditions, and our [grounds maintenance in Central Texas](grounds-maintenance) crew handles that shredding. Walk each pasture at least once a year and deal with new brush while it is small.

Healthy grass helps too. Good grass cover competes with seedlings, and sensible grazing keeps that cover strong. Properly cleared and maintained land is healthier, more valuable and more enjoyable to own.
